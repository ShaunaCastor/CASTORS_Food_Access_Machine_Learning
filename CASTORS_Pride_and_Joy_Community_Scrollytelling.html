We explore employment conditions, transportation access, and driving distance to SNAP-authorized food retailers.

Together with local organizations, these findings can support thoughtful conversations about food access and potential recovery partnerships.

Frame 01 / 07

Every Community Has a Story

Every community has a story, and every neighborhood is more than a statistic. Our research begins with 54 Census tracts across Trumbull County, Ohio, including Warren and surrounding communities. These geographic areas help us explore how employment, transportation, and access to food retailers vary throughout the region. Behind every data point is a community with its own strengths, relationships, and resources.

Frame 02 / 07

Understanding Economic Conditions

Employment opportunities are an important part of household economic stability. When we examine unemployment across Trumbull County, we see that economic conditions are not identical everywhere. Understanding these differences can help community organizations consider where additional research and outreach may be useful. Unemployment is only one part of the picture, and it does not define a community or the people who live there.

Frame 03 / 07

The Transportation Connection

Access to food is not only about whether a store exists. It can also depend on whether people have reliable transportation to reach it. Our research adds information about households without vehicles to better understand how economic and transportation conditions may overlap. This helps us consider how food delivery, community partnerships, and existing transportation resources might support more convenient access to food.

Frame 04 / 07

How Far Is Food?

Geography adds another important perspective. USDA data allow us to examine the share of residents living beyond a selected driving-distance threshold from SNAP-authorized retailers. The results show that food retail accessibility varies across Census tracts. Distance alone does not tell us whether food is affordable, nutritious, or convenient to obtain, but it can help guide conversations about local food-access opportunities.

Frame 05 / 07

Learning from the Data

Using employment, transportation, and retailer-access information, we developed a decision-tree model to estimate SNAP household participation across the study area. The model provides another way to explore relationships among the indicators. Its initial predictive accuracy is limited, so these estimates should not be treated as definitive measures of community food insecurity. Instead, the results help us identify questions that deserve closer examination alongside local knowledge and experience.

Frame 06 / 07

Exploring Recovery Partnerships

Alongside this analysis, we have begun identifying businesses and organizations that may offer opportunities for future food recovery partnerships. Our earlier mapping identified potential sources around Warren and neighboring communities, but their available surplus and donation capacity have not yet been verified. The next step is to explore whether existing resources, willing partners, and community organizations could collaborate to strengthen local food-access efforts.

Frame 07 / 07

Stronger Together

Every community brings something valuable to the table. Our findings provide a starting point for bringing together geographic research, local organizations, businesses, and community voices. Through collaboration with partners such as Pride & Joy Today Foundation, this work can help guide conversations about food recovery, transportation, and opportunities to strengthen existing food resources. The purpose of this research is not to define communities by their challenges, but to help identify practical opportunities for working together.

Research and sources

CASTORS Research Initiative · Trumbull County, Ohio. Source files: CASTORS_Predictive_Tree_Validated_Results.csv, CASTORS_Trumbull_Master_Python.csv, and CASTORS_Trumbull_Decision_Tree_Exploratory.csv. Master dataset references ACS 2020–2024 and USDA SRAM 2025.

Research dataset: 54 unique Census tracts. Each chart mark is a tract, not a household or business.

What we know

Measured tract indicators vary across the study area. A decision-tree model estimated household SNAP participation with limited predictive accuracy (cross-validated MAE 8.53 percentage points; pooled out-of-fold R² 0.058).

SNAP participation is not food insecurity. The uploaded records do not verify recoverable surplus, individual locations, three-mile buffers, or tract-to-business overlap.

What comes next

Listen to residents and community organizations. Verify prospective recovery partners and whether excess food is safe and available. Understand transportation and distribution capacity. Explore community-led collaboration.

Potential recovery leads should never be described as confirmed donors.

Data helps us see possibilities. Community partnerships help us explore what is possible in practice.
